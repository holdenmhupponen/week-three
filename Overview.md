I think Monday's exercises provided me with a more intimate and transparent look into the field of Archaeology and Digital Archaeology that I hadn't before considered. Like Huggett discusses, my previous perceptions of this type of work in society, filtered through a lack of personal experience, were given much more certainty that perhaps they should have. I viewed these fields and works as essentially unimpeachable? Now, if one were to have (and I speak in the past tense but this one experience has not entirely altered my own biases) questioned me on the fallibility of works, even those produced with high authority, I would likely come to admit its imperfections. Yet as an observer, I came into these works thinking that their conclusions were truly built on a nearly divine level of authority. As a Greek and Roman Studies major, I regularly encounter archaeological finds in my reading, and writing; since I am focused on the history, though, I often lose my critical rigor when it comes to these archaeological finds. I think, Oh, this paper was written by a bunch of doctors -- archaeologists, or chemists, etc. -- and therefore, it must be correct. Or, Oh, this conclusion was based on the result derived from this technological device. I would trust a calculator, right, so shouldn't I also blindly trust such archaeological devices? Perhaps not. Huggett even uses the example of photogrammetry to explain how the authority we provide to these devices gives us much more passive roles in the work. Similarly, when I was fiddling around with the laser system, now that I am thinking about it, I wasn't really engaging with the actual fragment I was given. I was interested in the green dots, and lack thereof. The lighting. The way I moved my hand one way or the other. I was interested in the actual fragment while watching others interact with it, but when it came to myself, I figured that the digital product would be just as good as the real thing. Then I submitted it. A progress bar filled up and the work I had done -- as a bowling ball dispenser hits a strike -- was submitted. What did it do? I understand the basics of it: the laser bounces off the object and back into the machine, which is catalogued onto a "cloud" of data stored in a three-dimensional model file. Still, though, the "black box" as Dennis puts it remained mostly oblique to me. Sure, I created a model, but I really just pressed a button so that a machine would perform a series of elaborate tasks for me. In the future, I want to ensure my role as an active actor. To not assume a digital tool or product will work simply because it is a digital tool or product. To channel the archaeologist within me and interrogate the processes I am undertaking in my efforts.
